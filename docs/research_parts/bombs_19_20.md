# Research part: bombs 19 (Soviet Test No. 219) and 20 (Tsar Bomba / AN602)

Scope: public historical, visual and effects data only (date, yield, burst height, fireball, cloud, timing,
lighting, outer dimensions, paint). No weapon-design data is collected here.

Status legend:
- **MEASURED**: instrument value or official record (test catalogue, seismic origin time, documented report by the test organisation).
- **ESTIMATED**: derived by scaling / physics formulas from `docs/02_PHYSICS_REFERENCE.md`, or a single soft source.
- **DISPUTED**: sources disagree; a reasoned game value is given.
- **COMPUTED**: astronomical calculation (Sun position) done for this research.

### Disputes resolved (Test 219)
1. **Date 24 vs 22 Dec**: 24 Dec is supported by the official catalogue (S7), Khalturin's appendix (S5), Johnston (S6), the CMR-based list (S14) and the contemporary US intelligence table (S16). 22 Dec comes from one weak article (S15). Game: **24 Dec 1962, 14:11 MSK**.
2. **Yield 24.2 vs 20 Mt**: 24.2 Mt is the Soviet instrument-based official figure; Western estimates spread from ~20 Mt (Johnston) to 26 Mt (US 1963). The official number sits inside that spread. Game: **24.2 Mt**.
3. **Burst height**: 3,750 m official vs 3,505 m (US 1963 estimate). Game: **3,750 m**.
4. **Delivery**: air drop, not ICBM (S15 is wrong).

### Visual signature (Test 219)
The scene is the polar night over the snow of Novaya Zemlya: black sky with stars, a faint deep-blue twilight band
low in the south-west, white snow lit only by starlight. Everything that follows is lit by the explosion itself.
- **0 s**: blue-white flash fills the sky; the whole snow field and any clouds within hundreds of km go instantly white, then orange; long shadows of hills point away from the burst.
- **0–0.4 s**: shock-front sphere grows to ≈ 3.5 km; at ≈ 0.4 s the thermal minimum: a dull orange-red opaque ball with a darker centre.
- **0.4–4.5 s**: the ball brightens again to blinding white (second maximum at ≈ 4.5 s) and grows to ≈ 6 km across; centre at 3.75 km, so its bottom comes within ≈ 0.7–0.8 km of the snow.
- **≈ 3 s**: shock reaches the ground below; a ring of snow dust kicks up and runs outward; at ≈ 5–6 s the reflected shock strikes the bottom of the fireball and flattens it (shape: Dominic Bighorn photo). In −20 to −30 °C dry air a Wilson condensation shell is unlikely or very faint (low absolute humidity) — skip it or show only a faint flash.
- **5–30 s**: colour goes white → yellow (≈ 10 s) → yellow-orange (≈ 20 s) → orange-red (≈ 45 s); the ball rises (top ≈ 9 km at 15 s) and rolls into a toroid; the snow landscape is lit orange over a radius of 300+ km.
- **≈ 40 s**: luminous dome top ≈ 25 km; mushroom forms; a thin pale stem of condensation and lifted snow, no dark dust.
- **50–90 s**: glow fades to dull red, then dark red-brown (nitrogen oxides); after that the cloud is a dark silhouette, visible only where it blocks stars or the twilight band; any residual reddish inner glow fades by ≈ 2 min.
- **2–10 min**: the dark cloud climbs to ≈ 53 km and spreads into a two-tier cap ≈ 72 km / 55 km wide. It stays unlit (Sun −11.5°), so render it as a faint grey-blue shape picked out by the twilight sky and starlight; optional camera exposure boost.
- **Shock**: the snow-dust ring reaches 10 km at ≈ 14 s, 20 km at ≈ 37 s, 50 km at ≈ 2 min.
- **Ground zero**: no crater; a circle several km wide where the snow is melted and the rock glazed (same as Tsar Bomba witnesses described).

### Disputes resolved (Tsar Bomba)
1. **Fireball "8 km wide / 10.5 km tall"**: 8 km is the DIAMETER (radius 4 km), and 10.5 km is the ALTITUDE of the fireball's top after it rose to the bomber's level, not the fireball height. Physics (megaton-corrected Glasstone radius 3.9 km) agrees with 8 km. The 4.6 km figure is a formula number (Pravda calls it diameter, Mail.ru calls it radius) and should not be used.
2. **Ground contact**: no contact; reflected shock flattened the bottom (render a flat, slightly concave underside a few hundred metres above the snow).
3. **Cloud height 64 vs 67 km**: both come from participants (Adamsky & Smirnov 1994: 64 km; VNIIEF Veselov/Veselovsky: 67 km). Render an overshoot to 67 km at ≈ 8–10 min, settling at ≈ 64 km.
4. **Cap widths**: two tiers, 95 km (upper) and 70 km (lower), from the VNIIEF account; consistent with Wellerstein's ~60 mi. The upper tier is the wider one.
5. **"Base 40 km"**: misreading of Pravda's "40 km in diameter"; use as the cap width at ≈ 2–3 min, not a base.
6. **Timing**: release 11:30:20, burst 11:33:28 MSK (08:33:28 UT); dome 30 km at 40 s; full height and width within ≈ 10 min.
7. **Weather / time of day**: overcast with two cloud layers; Sun only 2.3° above the southern horizon at local noon.
8. **Yield**: 50 Mt for the game (official), with 57–58 Mt noted as Western/early estimates.

### Visual signature (Tsar Bomba)
Setting: late-October Arctic noon on Novaya Zemlya: snow-covered mountains and coast, grey Barents Sea, a thick
overcast deck below the burst (tops ~2–4 km) and a broken upper layer; the Sun hangs only 2° above the southern
horizon, so the light is low, warm and golden, with very long shadows. A white Tu-95V drops the bomb from
10.5 km; a huge pale parachute lowers it for 188 s.
- **0 s (burst at 4.0 km)**: blinding blue-white flash; the overcast below lights up from inside and turns translucent ("a sea of light" under the aircraft, per the cameraman in S1).
- **0–0.56 s**: shock sphere expands to ≈ 4.6–5.5 km diameter (breakaway, S13 caption: ≈ 5.5 km at separation); at ≈ 0.55 s the thermal minimum: an orange-red opaque ball with a darker core.
- **0.56–6 s**: re-brightens to white (second maximum ≈ 6 s) and grows to ≈ 8 km across (centre 4 km, so its bottom nearly touches the ground). A thin condensation shell may flash around it if the humid autumn air allows (as in the Dominic Bighorn photo).
- **≈ 3 s**: shock hits the ground below; a white snow/dust ring races outward over the snow (Mach stem forms beyond ~4 km from ground zero).
- **6–8 s**: reflected shock slams into the underside: the ball gets a flat, slightly dished bottom and is pushed upward; it never touches the ground.
- **8–20 s**: from the aircraft it looks like a "huge bright orange ball" rising out of a gap in the clouds, slowly and silently (S1): colour white → yellow → bright orange; the top climbs to ≈ 10.5 km (the bomber's altitude) by ≈ 15–20 s; it punches through the cloud deck, which is blown out in a widening circle around it.
- **20–40 s**: the ball becomes a fiery red-orange dome rolling into a toroid with a dark-mottled surface (compare Housatonic photos); a pale column of condensation and lifted snow rises underneath to ≈ 10 km; at ≈ 40 s the glowing dome top is at ≈ 30 km and the mushroom forms; the glowing cloud is ≈ 15–20 km wide.
- **40–70 s**: glow fades from orange-red to dull red, then red-brown (nitrogen oxides); the flash and glow last a bit over a minute in total.
- **1–3 min**: cloud top ≈ 40–45 km; cap ≈ 40 km wide; lit by the low southern Sun: warm white-pink sunlit side, blue-grey shadow side; stem 10–20 km wide, mostly hidden below the overcast.
- **3–10 min**: the cloud climbs through the stratosphere into the mesosphere: ≈ 67 km at 8–10 min (overshoot), then settles toward ≈ 64 km; the cap spreads into two tiers, ≈ 95 km (upper, flatter, wider) and ≈ 70 km (lower); visible from 800 km.
- **Shock on the ground**: 10 km at ≈ 12 s, 20 km at ≈ 34 s, Severny (55 km) at ≈ 2 min, 100 km at ≈ 4 min; it caught the escaping Tu-95V at 115 km.
- **Ground zero after**: no crater; a levelled, glazed, snow-free area with shiny melted rock edges.
